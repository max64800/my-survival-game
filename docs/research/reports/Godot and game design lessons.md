# Keep caves fast, fights fair, demo ready

## In plain words

**Speed (making the game run smoothly)**
- A **frame** is one picture the game draws. **Frame rate (FPS)** is how many pictures appear each second. Our fight room that runs at 12 FPS is the number to beat.
- A **draw call** is the computer asking the graphics chip to draw one thing. Fewer asks means a faster game.
- Godot can bundle many copies of one thing into one ask, but only if they share one paint file (a **material**). Every brick piece should point to one shared paint file, not its own copy.
- Shadows are the most expensive light trick. A light that casts shadows in all directions costs about six extra drawings. Crystals should use cheaper lights with no shadows.
- Do not bake light in advance. Baked light takes minutes to redo, and digging changes the cave.
- Creatures you cannot see should pause their animation.
- Measure speed in an **exported** game (the finished build). The editor gives misleading numbers.

**Working with the AI helper**
- A **headless test** runs with no window. A robot can run it after every change. That is the cheapest safety net we can get.
- A **helper** (sub-agent) starts with an empty memory. Give it the exact files and the one question it must answer.
- Written rules are polite requests. A **hook** is a script the computer runs by itself. It can stop a bad save or commit before it happens.
- Scene files (.tscn) are text, but Godot writes ID codes inside them. Never fix those codes by hand. Keep one side of a merge, then open the project in Godot once.

**Co-op (playing together)**
- The **host** is the referee. Every player asks, and the host says yes or no.
- Never trust a number a player sends (their position, their ID) until the host has checked it.
- A **spawner** makes a thing appear on every screen. A **synchronizer** keeps sending a value as it changes.

**Fights and movement**
- A **wind-up** is the warning pose before an enemy hits. One detailed analysis of Dark Souls 3 says the warning plus the swing should last at least about one third of a second.
- **I-frames** are the moments when a dodge cannot be hurt. The number is only useful after you convert it to real time.
- **Coyote time** is a short grace period to jump after you walk off a ledge. A **jump buffer** remembers your jump press for a moment before you land.

**Guiding players and dungeons**
- A **weenie** is a big, easy-to-see landmark that pulls a player forward. Underground, a glowing crystal or a tall stone column can do this job.
- One rule from a famous open-world game: something should call to the player at least every 30 seconds of walking.
- **Key and lock**: one item found in a dungeon opens that dungeon's boss door.

**Sound, playtests, scope and the demo**
- Start with no music. Add music only at two or three big moments.
- Use one voice for all dwarves, with a few short lines and a cooldown so they never pile up.
- In a playtest, watch and do not help. Write down what the player does, not what they say.
- A **Next Fest** is Steam's big free-demo event. Our demo deadline (Steam's review) is January 18, 2027, and the owner must decide this now.

## The short answer

The best use of this research is to fix three things first, in this order: enemy warnings that are long enough to read, a small set of cheap rendering rules that keep the cave fast, and an automatic headless test that every AI-written change must pass. The sources agree on the first two in principle, but the exact numbers for our game are guesses until we test them. The Steam Next Fest question is a calendar decision, not a research one: a stable 15-to-25-minute demo must pass Steam's review by January 18, 2027, or the team should aim for the June 2027 fest. Most of the numbers below come from official Godot documentation or one fetched analysis, and each is labelled so the owner knows which ones to trust.

**How sure is each claim?** Each finding carries one tag. **[Sourced]** means an official doc or a fetched primary-like analysis. **[Secondary]** means a blog, wiki, forum or search summary, which points the way but needs a check. **[Guess]** means our own rule of thumb, to test in play.

## Keep frames fast before adding more beauty

Godot 4.7 uses the Forward+ renderer (the drawing engine that handles many lights), and the official docs describe how to keep it fast. The key fact is that **[Sourced]** Forward+ automatically bundles plain 3D meshes that share one mesh and one material, but only when the material is opaque or uses alpha-scissor (a cut-out, like leaves) or alpha-hash. Alpha-blended (see-through) materials are not bundled and are sorted back to front ([Godot: Optimizing 3D performance](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/performance/optimizing_3d_performance.html)). The docs give a 20,000-object example that renders much faster when those objects share 100 materials instead of 20,000 ([Godot: GPU optimization](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/performance/gpu_optimization.html)). For us, this means every brick, cut-stone and plank piece should reference one shared material per kind, with tints done by per-instance colour. Glass or glow materials should be cut-out or unlit where possible. The docs do not measure the gain, so the first test is a before-and-after count in the fight room.

Grass, crystals, roots and similar clutter should use MultiMesh, which draws thousands of copies in one call. The catch is **[Sourced]** that MultiMesh has all-or-none visibility, so the whole group is either drawn or skipped. Split large clutter into per-chunk MultiMeshes so the camera can skip the far chunks ([Godot: Using MultiMesh](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/performance/using_multimesh.html)). The existing voxel plan already chooses per-chunk MultiMesh, so this confirms that choice.

Lights are the costliest rendering item after the terrain. **[Sourced]** An omni light with cube shadows draws the scene six times, a dual-paraboloid omni light draws it twice, and a spot light draws it once. Directional (sun-like) shadows use four splits by default, and every extra shadowed directional light lowers the detail of all shadows, because they share one atlas (a shared shadow image) ([Godot: Lights and shadows](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/lights_and_shadows.html)). The same page says turning off shadows on as many lights as possible is the best improvement. So the crystals should be spot lights or omni lights with shadows off, and the dwarf's lantern should be the only shadowed light. The existing plan's crystal fade distances (12 to 35 m) are plausible but unsourced **[Guess]**.

Baked lighting is the wrong tool for this game. **[Sourced]** LightmapGI lightmaps "can't be modified at all" after baking, and a bake takes minutes or longer ([Godot: Using LightmapGI](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/global_illumination/using_lightmap_gi.html)). A dug cave changes every few minutes, so baked light would be wrong almost at once. The docs give no cost number for SDFGI or VoxelGI, so **[Guess]** we should not turn them on in the cave without a benchmark.

Stutter (a sudden hitch) is a different problem from low FPS. **[Sourced]** Since Godot 4.4, Forward+ builds its shader pipelines at load time, but only for features listed in the docs, and first-use features cause hitches during play. The docs say the fix is to render a scene using those features early, even off-screen in a SubViewport (a hidden mini-view) ([Godot: Reducing stutter from shader compilations](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/performance/pipeline_compilations.html)). So the loading screen should draw one of each piece, crystal, creature and glow material, plus a spot light, once. Big ruin and creature scenes should use `load_threaded_request` (load in the background) and `load_threaded_get` only after the status says done ([Godot: Background loading](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/io/background_loading.html)). The docs give no numbers for the gain, but the owner's 110 to 150 second load is the project's own measure, so the loading-screen warm-up should be timed against it.

Voxel Tools has its own knobs, and most matter for digging. **[Sourced]** The thread settings (`voxel/threads/count/ratio_over_maximum`, `margin_below_maximum`) decide how many CPU cores the terrain uses. Leave at least one core free for the game, and they need a restart to apply. The main-thread time budget defaults to about 8 ms per frame. Bulk edits (`do_sphere()`, `do_box()`, or building a buffer and calling `paste()`) are much faster than looping over single voxels in GDScript. Mesh blocks of 32 reduce draw calls but make edits slower. Editor runs of multithreaded GDScript are much slower than exported builds ([Voxel Tools: Performance](https://voxel-tools.readthedocs.io/en/latest/performance/)). For us: set the thread ratio in the settings menu, check it with `VoxelEngine.get_stats()`, make every dig a bulk call (the co-op dig command path too), and keep the 16 block size for digging. Using 32 for distant blocks is **[Guess]** and not verified.

Physics is less settled. The built-in Jolt physics engine is intended to be faster and more stable, but **[Secondary]** one community benchmark shows Rapier beating Jolt by 4.0 times in a 3D query test, and a second table is unlabelled ([Godot forum: Physics engine comparison](https://forum.godotengine.org/t/physics-engine-comparison-rapier-vs-godot-vs-box2d-3d-vs-jolt/142786)). The Godot pull request also warns that changing the shape of a body already in the scene can make things slower. So debris should be made with its final box or sphere shape and never reshaped. The existing plan says Jolt became the default in 4.6, but the 4.7 upgrade guide does not confirm that, so check the 4.6 release notes before relying on it. **[Guess]** The cave collision cost must be measured on a real dug cave, not on a synthetic test.

GDScript speed advice is mostly well-known and cheap. **[Sourced]** Typed code uses faster instructions when the types are known, and the editor can warn about untyped declarations. The docs give no speed numbers, and an independent thesis found static typing effects vary, sometimes slower in edge cases ([Godot: Static typing](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/scripting/gdscript/static_typing.html); [Twente thesis](https://essay.utwente.nl/107857/1/Goryachkin_BA_EEMCS.pdf)). The **[Sourced]** 4.7 upgrade changed the packed-array setter (it no longer fires for the whole array when one element changes), which may help the cave planners and MultiMesh writes ([Godot 4.7 upgrade guide](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/migrating/upgrading_to_godot_4.7.html)). The CPU docs say removing a node from the tree can beat hiding it, so far creatures and sleeping debris should be removed or swapped for plain meshes, not just hidden ([Godot: CPU optimization](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/performance/cpu_optimization.html)). For creatures in the fight room, the likely big cost is animation. `VisibleOnScreenEnabler3D` only checks the camera's view, so a creature hidden behind rock would still animate. **[Guess]** a line-of-sight check or a lower animation rate for far creatures is needed.

The docs' Profiler is the first tool. **[Sourced]** Use the Self column to find the function's own cost, and compare frame time with physics and idle time to tell CPU from GPU ([Godot: The Profiler](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/scripting/debug/the_profiler.html)). Add the Surface and Mesh pipeline counters to the scripted perf log, since a spike there after loading is a real hitch ([Godot: Reducing stutter](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/performance/pipeline_compilations.html)).

## Working faster with an AI helper without breaking the game

The biggest speed gain for our build is not in the engine. It is in the feedback loop. A set of headless tests that fail loudly makes every AI-written change safer. **[Secondary]** gdUnit4 is the test framework with the most current support: its v7 master is listed as compatible with Godot 4.7, and its official GitHub Action runs headless and produces JUnit and HTML reports ([gdUnit4 README](https://github.com/godot-gdunit-labs/gdUnit4/blob/master/README.md?plain=1); [gdUnit4-action](https://github.com/MikeSchulze/gdUnit4-action)). Our custom `tests/run_tests.gd` already prints only FAILs and a summary, which is a good base. The action for us is **[Guess]** a small trial: move one or two test files to gdUnit4 and keep the custom runner until the trial is judged. Either way, the test run must return a non-zero exit code on failure. Check this once by making a test fail on purpose, because a runner that prints FAIL but exits zero will pass broken builds silently.

A CI workflow (a robot that runs on every push) is worth adding now. **[Secondary]** The community pattern is: install Godot 4.7.2 headless, run the import once so the `.godot/` cache exists, run the tests, and fail the job on any FAIL ([Rubbley issue #31](https://github.com/snackpackgames/Rubbley/issues/31); [godot-ci Docker setup](https://github.com/nicolanore/godot-ci)). This catches breakage when several Claude sessions push at once. The source is community guides, so the first run is the real test.

Scene files need care. **[Sourced]** Godot 4.4 moved UIDs (unique ID codes) to `.uid` files and into `.tscn`/`.tres` files, and the design expects conflicts to be solved by taking one file ([Godot 4.4 UID article](https://godotengine.org/article/uid-changes-coming-to-godot-4-4/)). A duplicate-UID bug is still open, with only a user workaround ([godot issue #102490](https://github.com/godotengine/godot/issues/102490)). The safe rule: never hand-edit an ID, keep one side of a merge, then open the project in Godot once and save. Commit every `.uid` file. A `.gitattributes` merge rule is **[Secondary]** a possible time-saver but should be tested on a scratch branch first.

Two Godot 4.7 notes help the owner's own tooling. **[Secondary]** The MCP (the bridge that lets the AI see the running game) servers differ: one takes screenshots of the running game, another of the editor window. Check which one our `godot` MCP uses before trusting its pictures for a visual check ([fernforestgames/mcp-server-godot-editor](https://github.com/fernforestgames/mcp-server-godot-editor)). Keep the debugger and error-reading tools as the main source of truth, and treat screenshots as a supplement. **[Sourced]** Godot 4.7 also adds device IDs to input events, which could help controller support later ([Godot 4.7 summary](https://godotawesome.com/godot-4-7-features/)).

Rules for the AI itself come from Anthropic's own guidance. **[Sourced]** CLAUDE.md is guidance Claude may decide not to apply, while hard constraints belong in permissions or hooks ([Claude Code best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). The owner's "no personal paths" and "close test games" rules are hard rules, so each should become a hook: a pre-commit check that rejects personal paths, user names and `.env`-style files, and an end-of-session check that no `claude=` test game is still running. For helpers, the same guidance says a subagent sees none of the conversation, so each brief must list the files, the one question and the return shape (for example `file:line` plus one line each) ([crystl.dev on subagents](https://crystl.dev/blog/claude-code-subagents-best-practices/)). Fan-out only saves chat space when the answer is short and fixed. **[Guess]** One caution from our own rules: `export_presets.cfg` is the file CI needs, but it can hold export paths. Check it for personal paths before committing it.

## Co-op: the host decides and the sync stays small

Godot's own co-op pattern is clear enough to use, though the source here is community guides, not the official docs. **[Secondary]** A MultiplayerSpawner makes nodes exist on every peer, and a MultiplayerSynchronizer keeps changing values in step. Spawning should start on the authority (the host). Use one spawner per group (dropped items, dug stations, creatures), give spawned objects their starting data in the spawn function, and sync only values that change over time ([Godot Forum thread](https://forum.godotengine.org/t/leveraging-godots-multiplayer-spawner-synchroniser-appropriately/69058); [Andrew Davis, Godot multiplayer tips](https://jonandrewdavis.com/drafts/draft-of-godot-network-tips/)). Short-lived objects like thrown rocks can skip the synchronizer and move by their starting velocity.

With GodotSteam, the lobby owner is peer ID 1, the server and default authority ([GodotSteam MultiplayerPeer tutorial](https://godotsteam.com/tutorials/multiplayer_peer/)). A third-party write-up says a client can send a peer ID the plugin does not check ([Axeghost write-up](https://axeghost.offprint.app/a/3mzhxoqpnh423-any-networked-co-player-can-act-as-the-server-in-a-godotsteam-game)). That is a single secondary source, so it must be checked in the GodotSteam issue tracker. Either way, the rule for us is the same: the host checks every client request (who is asking, and is the action allowed) before anything changes. The game already follows this host-approves pattern, so the job is to make it a checked rule rather than an assumption.

The release risk is simpler. **[Secondary]** One developer's Steam postmortem says a build ran on their PC but failed on a clean Windows machine, and they ran the export and upload by hand before automating ([Gamineai postmortem](https://gamineai.com/blog/i-shipped-a-godot-game-to-steam-full-postmortem)). For us, that means a written release checklist before the first Steam build: export from the command line, copy to a machine with no Godot installed, start a host and a client, and confirm save and join both work. The save design itself (host leaving, atomic saves, backups, Steam Cloud) is already in `docs/research/coop-and-saves.md` and is not repeated here. **[Sourced]** If the game uses soft bodies or Area3D overlap checks, the 4.7 upgrade changed both under Jolt, so re-check those first ([Godot 4.7 upgrade guide](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/migrating/upgrading_to_godot_4.7.html)).

## Fights and movement: warn first, then hit

The owner's "no wind-up" complaint has a number behind it. **[Sourced]** In one fetched analysis of Dark Souls 3, an enemy's attack signal and attack together must last at least **340 milliseconds**, and the bait pose at least 240 milliseconds. The same analysis models the player's reaction chain at roughly 240 milliseconds in total (perception about 100, thinking about 70, pressing about 70) ([Game Developer, Anatomy of an enemy attack in Dark Souls 3](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/anatomy-of-an-enemy-attack-in-dark-souls-3)). At 60 frames per second, 340 milliseconds is about 20 frames. The analysis also says the damaging hit lasts only 2 to 4 frames, so a long warning with a short hit reads as fair, and a short warning with a long hit reads as unfair. The rule for our enemies is the warning length, not the hit length. This is a floor from one analysis, not a tested rule, so the first test is a silent playtest on the current worst enemy with the warning set to the 340 millisecond floor.

Dodge invincibility needs a conversion warning. The Souls wiki counts i-frames in 1/30 second: 9, 11, 13 and 15 frames for different rolls ([Fextralife, Agility](https://darksouls2.wiki.fextralife.com/Agility); [Dark Souls wiki, Rolling](https://darksouls.fandom.com/wiki/Rolling)). That is about **300 to 500 milliseconds** of invincibility. The Dark Souls 3 analysis gives **433 milliseconds** for a medium roll, starting from the first frame. The wiki sources disagree with each other on some rolls, so treat the range as a guide. The earlier draft of this research suggested starting at 10 to 13 frames at 60 frames per second. That is only 167 to 217 milliseconds, which is about half the wiki range, so it was a conversion error. **[Guess]** Start the dodge's invincibility between about 0.3 and 0.4 seconds (roughly 18 to 24 frames at 60 FPS), start it on the first animation frame, and test by feel. Add a short vulnerable tail at the end of the roll as a boss difficulty lever, which the wiki calls instability frames.

Jump forgiveness is cheap and clearly useful. **[Sourced]** Celeste's designer describes coyote time and jump buffering as "a short time" with no number, and gives a 50 percent gravity cut at the top of the jump ([Maddy Thorson, Celeste and forgiveness](https://maddymakesgames.com/articles/celeste_and_forgiveness/)). A community source puts coyote time at about 5 frames **[Secondary]** ([GameRant](https://gamerant.com/celeste-coyote-time-mechanic-platforming-impact-hidden-mechanics/)). For a heavy dwarf, start with a short coyote window and jump buffer in the 5 to 6 frame range **[Guess]** and test.

The Titanfall 2 designers give the most useful feel rules we found, and they are **[Sourced]** from a fetched interview. Players did not trust the wall-run and jumped off too early; training sections and enforced early wall runs fixed it, and the speed boost gave a reason to use the move. A slide that cannot turn easily, with a small starting boost, fits a heavy body. Tilting the view toward a wall before a wall run helps players anticipate it. A view spring after a long fall, scaled by fall height, adds weight ([Game Developer, Titanfall 2 controls interview](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/designer-interview-getting-i-titanfall-i-s-controls-just-right)). The melee note already covers hit-stop, so this adds only the rule that each move should reward the player with a small visible benefit.

For boss health with 1 to 4 players, there is no developer number to copy. Community posts claim +50 percent health per extra player in one Dark Souls thread ([Steam forum, Dark Souls](https://steamcommunity.com/app/211420/discussions/0/357288572113450185)), and Don't Starve Together players suggested three boss versions by player count ([Klei forum](https://forums.kleientertainment.com/forums/topic/169689-boss-multiplayer-difficulty-scaling/)). Both are **[Secondary]**. The useful lesson is that complaints come from both sides (bosses "melt" with four players, or co-op is too easy). **[Guess]** For our game, scale the immunity windows and the chain limit per monster, and scale health only modestly. The owner's "too brutal" complaint is more likely about damage per hit and stun chains than health, so tune those first.

## Guiding players and building dungeons without a quest list

The landmark rule is the best-sourced idea in this area. **[Secondary]** Sucker Punch's landmark system, described in a Game Developer article on the GDC 2021 talk, splits landmarks into tall **Flags** seen from afar and ground-level **Breadcrumbs** that work in dense forest. Their density rule is that the player should see something calling to them **every 30 seconds or less** while travelling ([Game Developer, A taxonomy of weenies](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/a-taxonomy-of-weenies-the-landmarks-that-define-i-ghost-of-tsushima-i-)). Breath of the Wild used the biggest terrain shapes as visual markers, and moved towers off straight routes so exploring stayed optional ([Game Developer, Breath of the Wild lessons](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/5-design-lessons-learned-from-i-the-legend-of-zelda-breath-of-the-wild-i-)). Underground there is no sky, so the Flag becomes a glowing crystal or tall stone column that can be seen from the next room, and the Breadcrumb becomes a wall marking or small light at every fork. The 30-second rule translates to **[Guess]** "within the next turn" in short cave passages. Test it in the first zone by counting how often a playtester stops and asks where to go.

Guidance can also come from the reward loop, not from a list. **[Secondary]** Valheim ties each boss to a material: after the boss falls, the next material unlocks, which gives players a reason to explore and a clear next step ([Windows Central boss guide](https://www.windowscentral.com/valheim-boss-guide-how-find-summon-and-defeat-every-boss); [Twinfinite](https://twinfinite.net/pc/4-features-that-set-valheim-apart-from-other-survival-games/3/)). For us, beating Orrumhal should unlock the next material the player is already short of. Subnautica and Core Keeper are cited for the same idea (a visible target and freedom, no checklist), though those sources are summaries, not fetched. Avoid a written quest list.

Loot needs two kinds of rules. **[Secondary]** Scarcity is usually set by an item's power, with stronger items later or rarer ([Game Developer, Emotions and randomness in loot drops](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/emotions-and-randomness-loot-drops)). Pure random drops can give players gear they already own, and if every drop is exactly what the player needs the game becomes predictable ([Game Developer, Bunker Punks loot](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/making-loot-drops-more-engaging-in-bunker-punks)). A two-step roll also hides the real odds: a 50 percent slot chance times a 20 percent item chance is a 10 percent real chance ([Palworld wiki](https://palworld.wiki.gg/wiki/Treasure_Chests)). For us, keep the best gear and recipe pages in large dungeons or ruins behind a key or boss, use drop-table logic so a dungeon never gives a tier the player already has, and put a pity rule on any single item that blocks progress. Treat a recipe page as a gate, not a random find.

Dungeon design is supported mostly by level-design analysis, not numbers. **[Secondary]** Jabu Jabu's Belly is easier to navigate than the Water Temple because its order of obstacles is clear, even though its rooms are simpler ([Game Developer, Zelda level design](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/take-a-close-look-at-the-level-design-of-some-exceptional-i-zelda-i-games)). Deep Rock Galactic mixes hand-made cave pieces with random ones, and the designers say the best surprises come from pieces joining in unplanned ways ([PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/deep-rock-galactic/deep-rock-galactic-unreal-engine-4)). The advice that follows: keep each dungeon hand-made in layout, with one key, one loop back to the entrance, and an ordered set of obstacles, and vary the order and key placement across the 12 dungeons per zone instead of building 12 new layouts. The three sizes (small, medium, large) and their contents are **[Guess]** our own proposal. Each trap should be visible before it hurts, because the Zelda analysis treats obstacles as readable teaching tools.

For point-of-interest density, the warning is that more is not always better. **[Secondary]** Ubisoft's Assassin's Creed Shadows developers aimed for no point of interest every 50 metres, pushing back against icon-heavy maps ([GamesRadar](https://www.gamesradar.com/games/assassin-s-creed/assassins-creed-shadows-forced-ubisoft-to-craft-the-crap-out-of-its-new-style-open-world-ensuring-you-wont-have-points-of-interest-every-50-meters)). The owner's "world too small" complaint is more likely about looping and forks than about empty rooms. **[Guess]** More loops, more forks and places seen from far will fix the feel more than more rooms. Every placed landmark still follows the project's placement rule: nothing the world grows may stand inside a built place or on its paths.

## Sound, playtests, scope and the Next Fest demo

Music should start silent. **[Secondary]** Minecraft's soundtrack has long gaps, and the commentary says the sudden return of music makes an ordinary moment feel special ([Killscreen](https://www.killscreen.com/quiet-importance-minecraft-soundtrack/)). The composer C418 said the silence lets players hear the world, though that quote comes from a secondary podcast summary ([GameRant, Twenty Thousand Hertz](https://gamerant.com/minecraft-c418-twenty-thousand-hertz-podcast-interview/)). **[Sourced]** Godot 4.3 added `AudioStreamInteractive` (switches between clips on a beat, bar or clip end) and `AudioStreamSynchronized` (plays several streams in time with separate volumes) ([Godot 4.3 release notes](https://godotengine.org/releases/4.3/index.html)). The plan: one calm track and one tense track, the same length and tempo, joined by `AudioStreamSynchronized`, with the tense volume rising when a monster is alert or chasing. Switch only on a beat so it never sounds broken. Music is only for two or three moments: the first real threat, the big cave reveal, and the home base. Give players a music-off option, since fan mods for Valheim show that is asked for ([Thunderstore, Valheim Music Plus](https://thunderstore.io/c/valheim/p/Emberskald/Valheim_Music_Plus/v/1.13.0/)). Keep the music real recorded instruments, per the owner's no-synth rule. Ducking (lowering music under a voice line) was not sourced; test it by ear.

Dwarf voices should be few. **[Sourced]** Deep Rock Galactic uses one shared voice for all dwarves, with lines triggered by events ([DRG wiki, Voicelines](https://deeprockgalactic.wiki.gg/wiki/Voicelines)). Its rare joke line "Karl" began at about a 1 percent chance, and the developers dropped one upsetting line from a spin-off ([TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/deep-rock-galactic-loot-bug-dwarves-writing/); [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/deep-rock-galactic-auto-battler-nixes-upsetting-die-like-your-mother-did-voice-line-so-as-to-not-remind-some-players-of-tragic-events-in-their-life)). For us: four to six short event lines per dwarf (hurt, effort, found something, help, low air, thanks), a cooldown of about 8 to 15 seconds **[Guess]**, one line at a time, and a 1 to 2 percent rare line. In co-op, each game plays the same line locally, and only the event name is sent. Voice sourcing is a separate legal check: read the AI voice tool's commercial terms, or record a real person with a signed release.

Playtesting works best silently. **[Secondary]** Do not help the tester, watch what they do, and ask why they chose each thing afterwards. Test one or two aspects per session ([gtstu.com guide](https://gtstu.com/playtest-indie-game-solo-developer/)). ## Conclusion: the feel of waiting matters most

The sources point to one pattern across every area: the game has to be readable before it can be fast or big. A warning that lasts a third of a second is the enemy's way of saying "I am about to hit," and a landmark every 30 seconds is the cave's way of saying "keep going." Both are cheap to build and cost nothing in frame rate. The rendering rules (shared materials, cheap lights, no baked light) keep the cave readable at speed, and a headless test keeps the AI from breaking it while changing things.

The main thing the research changes is the order of work. The conventional advice is to make the game beautiful and then test it. The evidence here says to make the warnings, the landmarks and the test runner first, because each one makes every later change cheaper to judge. The Next Fest question should not drive the feature list: a short, stable demo built from the cave opening is the only version that can meet January's review, and it also serves as the first proof that the game plays well.
