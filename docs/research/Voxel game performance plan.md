# Make the caves run fast everywhere

The game is already fast on a good PC: about **5 ms per frame (around 200 frames per second) on an RTX 3060 Ti**. The real problems are slow world loading and the missing safety net for weak PCs. Three fixes give the most for the least work. First, keep making cave planning cheaper and save the result, so a world never has to be planned twice. Second, add a graphics settings menu with a "Potato" preset that turns down render scale, shadows, fog and SSAO. Third, switch physics to Jolt and trim the rock debris. The owner's Horizon Zero Dawn idea, "only draw what you look at", is already built into Godot as frustum culling. The version that really pays off in a cave game is a different trick: hide whole cave sections the camera cannot see through tunnels, the way Minecraft does. That is a medium-term project, not a quick setting. Long term, the cave-shape maths should move out of GDScript into faster native code. That is the only path that makes loading close to instant.

## In plain words first

Think of the computer as a kitchen that has to cook one picture (a "frame") many times every second. Right now your kitchen cooks each picture in about 5 thousandths of a second (5 **milliseconds**, or "ms"). That is fast, about 200 pictures a second. To feel smooth, a game needs 60 pictures a second (about 16.7 ms each). A slow "potato" PC, like a cheap laptop, is maybe 4 to 6 times slower than yours. So the same picture could take it 20 to 30 ms. That is too slow, and it is why settings matter.

Some words used below:
- The **CPU** is the head chef. It decides what goes on the plate.
- The **GPU** is the painter. It paints the picture.
- A **draw call** is the chef handing the painter one job. Your game hands over about 1,100 jobs per picture.
- **Culling** means "skip things nobody can see." It is like not cooking food for guests who aren't there.
- A **thread** is an extra cook. Work split across threads finishes sooner.
- **Native code** (C++) is a much faster kind of program than GDScript, the language the game is written in now. Think of a power drill instead of a hand screwdriver.

The slowest part today is not the picture. It is the **loading**. Building a world takes about 80 seconds. The game plans every plant, crystal and root by asking "is this spot rock or air?" millions of times, and each question takes 40 millionths of a second in GDScript. That adds up fast. The planning already runs on a helper thread behind a moving loading screen, so the game no longer freezes. It is still slow, though.

About Horizon Zero Dawn: the famous clip where the world vanishes behind the camera shows something Godot **already does on its own**. Things outside your view are skipped. In a cave, the bigger win is skipping things that are **in front of you but behind solid rock**. Minecraft does this with a clever trick: it checks which tunnels connect, and it only draws cave rooms you could actually see into.

Here is what to do, in order:
1. **Now (days):** a graphics menu with presets, Jolt physics, fewer and simpler debris rocks, shorter "draw distances" for small things, and lights that switch off far away.
2. **Soon (weeks):** save each planned world to a file so the second load is quick, only build collision near players, a built-in speed test, and a "Safe mode" for very old PCs.
3. **Later (months):** Minecraft-style cave culling, the cave maths in fast native code, and smarter network sending of digs.

## The PC is not the bottleneck; loading and weak GPUs are

Start with where the time actually goes. On the dev PC the frame is about 4.6 to 5 ms, and the GPU accounts for about 3.9 ms of it. That leaves big headroom at 60 FPS. Moving the firefly swarms from per-frame GDScript to a GPU shader cut the frame from 11 to 12 ms down to about 5 ms. That matches Godot's guidance: anything updated every frame for "tens of thousands of instances" belongs on servers, shaders or MultiMesh, not nodes ([Godot: Optimization using Servers](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/performance/using_servers.html)). **Already done:** the firefly MultiMesh also has a `custom_aabb` that covers the whole swarm (`src/art/glow_swarms.gd`). Godot needs that box to cull correctly when a shader moves the instances ([MultiMesh class](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_multimesh.html)).

The potato target is set by the Steam survey. In October 2026, the **GTX 1650 (2.32%) and Intel Iris Xe integrated graphics (1.73%)** were still in the top 15 GPUs. **4 to 6 GB VRAM** covered about 10.7% of players, **8 GB of RAM** 7.5%, and **4-core CPUs** 12.4%. Half of all players use 1080p ([Steam Hardware Survey](https://store.steampowered.com/hwsurvey/)). Similar co-op survival games set honest minimums at that level. Valheim lists a quad-core CPU, 8 GB RAM and a GTX 950 ([Valheim on Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/892970/Valheim/)). The Steam Deck is the best "middle-low" test machine: 4 Zen 2 cores, a 1.6 TFLOPS GPU, and a 1280x800 screen ([Steam Deck specs](https://www.steamdeck.com/en/tech/deck)). A sensible target is **30 FPS at the Low preset on a GTX 1650 or Iris Xe at 1080p with 50 to 67% render scale, and 30 to 60 FPS on the Deck**. One caution: the claim that the dev PC is 4 to 6 times faster than those GPUs is an estimate, not a benchmark. The real number has to be measured on real hardware.

Renderer choice matters most at the very bottom. Godot's docs are blunt. Forward+ has the highest base cost and is "the slowest of the three" on old and low-end hardware, while Compatibility is the cheapest ([Godot: Overview of renderers](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/rendering/renderers.html)). Compatibility has costs of its own, though. It drops volumetric fog, SDFGI, SSR, SSIL, TAA, FSR2 and decals. It also limits each mesh to **8 omni plus 8 spot lights** ([same source](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/rendering/renderers.html)). In a cave lit by many glowing crystals, big terrain chunks would have lights popping on and off. So the main potato path should be **Forward+ with a Potato preset**. Compatibility should be a "Safe mode" fallback that at least starts the game. The renderer cannot change while the game runs. Start-up options such as `--rendering-method gl_compatibility` work in release builds ([Godot: Command line tutorial](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/editor/command_line_tutorial.html)). Since 4.4, Godot also falls back to OpenGL on its own when neither Vulkan nor D3D12 starts ([renderers](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/rendering/renderers.html)). Leave that switched on.

## A graphics menu is the biggest missing piece for potato PCs

The project has no graphics settings menu today. `src/art/ambience.gd` turns on volumetric fog and SSAO for everyone. The single strongest lever for weak GPUs is `Viewport.scaling_3d_scale`. A value of 0.5 at 1080p renders 0.52 megapixels instead of 2.07, which is about a quarter of the pixel work ([Godot: Resolution scaling](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/resolution_scaling.html)). There is a catch: "on very slow GPUs, FSR 1.0's cost may outweigh its benefit." So Potato should default to bilinear, and FSR 1 should be measured before it is used ([same source](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/resolution_scaling.html)). The project's `src/debug/perfcheck.gd` already flips scale between 0.5 and 1.0. That is the docs' recommended fill-rate test: if FPS jumps when resolution drops, the game is pixel-bound ([Godot: GPU optimization](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/performance/gpu_optimization.html)). **Partly done:** the test exists, but no player-facing setting does.

The official graphics-settings demo is a ready template. It sets `mesh_lod_threshold` to 8 / 4 / 2 / 1 / 0 from Very Low to Ultra. It applies shadows through `RenderingServer.directional_shadow_atlas_set_size()`, `get_viewport().positional_shadow_atlas_size` and the soft-shadow filter quality calls. It sets SSAO with `environment_set_ssao_quality(level, true, 0.5, 2, 50, 300)` and uses SSR step counts of 8/32/56 ([godot-demo-projects graphics_settings](https://github.com/godotengine/godot-demo-projects/blob/master/3d/graphics_settings/settings.gd)). Shadows are where most of the cost hides. A default omni cube shadow renders 6 times, dual paraboloid renders 2, and a spot light renders 1. The docs suggest a 2048 shadow map for low end instead of 4096, and 16-bit depth ([Godot: Lights and shadows](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/lights_and_shadows.html)). They also say "disabling shadows on as many lights and objects as possible is the best improvement" ([GPU optimization](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/performance/gpu_optimization.html)). Volumetric fog cost scales with Volume Size times Volume Depth. Plain depth and height fog works in every renderer and is cheaper ([Godot: Volumetric fog](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/volumetric_fog.html)).

| Setting | Potato | Low | Medium | High |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `scaling_3d_scale` / mode | 0.5 bilinear | 0.67 bilinear or FSR1 | 0.77 FSR1 | 1.0 (or FSR2 0.77) |
| Shadows | Lantern only, 1024, Hard | Lantern + 2-4 lights, 1024 | 2048 Soft Very Low | 4096 Soft Low |
| Volumetric fog | Off (depth/height fog) | Off | Small volume, filter off | On |
| SSAO / SSIL / SSR / SDFGI | All off | All off | SSAO low half-size | SSAO medium |
| `mesh_lod_threshold` | 8 | 4 | 2 | 1 |
| Decoration `visible_instance_count` | ~40% | ~60% | ~80% | All |
| Crystal light distance fade | ~12 m | ~18 m | ~25 m | ~35 m |
| AA | None | FXAA | FXAA/TAA | TAA or MSAA 2x |

The table's exact numbers are starting points to tune, not sourced values. On first launch, run a short hidden fly-through, pick a tier, drop one tier if FPS is short, and save the result. Unreal-style auto-detect works this way ([Tom Looman](https://tomlooman.com/auto-detect-graphics-settings-ue4)). `RenderingServer.get_video_adapter_type()` can flag an integrated GPU before the test runs ([Godot: RenderingServer](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_renderingserver.html)). Each option could show a small "costs a lot / a little" label, the way Gears Tactics' well-liked menu does ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/gears-tactics-has-the-best-graphics-option-menus-ive-ever-seen-and-every-game-should-copy-them)). That label also suits the owner's plain-language style.

## "Only draw what you look at" works, but caves need tunnel-aware culling

Horizon Zero Dawn's famous "view cone" clip shows frustum culling. Guerrilla's real system goes further. It tests 500K to 1.5M static instances on the GPU each frame against the previous frame's depth buffer, in about 1 to 2 ms, and calls the result "not perfect, but good enough" ([Decima Engine: Visibility in HZD](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/decima-engine-visibility-in-horizon-zero-dawn/82371923)). Godot does the frustum part on its own for every node, using its bounding box. The docs say a naive renderer might draw 10 to 100 times more than is visible ([Godot: Optimizing 3D performance](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/performance/optimizing_3d_performance.html)). So the owner's idea is **already running**. What helps further is applying HZD's lessons to how the scene is built: cull small things early, and keep objects small enough to cull.

That second point matters because a MultiMesh "is spatially indexed as a single object." A big spread-out MultiMesh draws every instance whenever any part of it is on screen ([MultiMesh class](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_multimesh.html)). It also picks one LOD for all its instances ([Godot: Mesh LOD](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/mesh_lod.html)). So decoration MultiMeshes should be split **per chunk or per cave room**. HZD's "size test" maps onto `visibility_range_end` with Fade Mode Disabled and a hysteresis margin, which is the cheapest fade ([Godot: Visibility ranges](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/visibility_ranges.html)). **Already done in many places:** clutter (45 to 60 m), root curtains (60 m), mushroom grass, cave life, cave water (45 m), glow fields (70 m) and labels all have visibility ranges with margins. **Still to do:** check that those MultiMeshes are chunked rather than cave-wide. Also give every crystal or decoration light `distance_fade_enabled`, which stops sending the light to the shader past begin + length ([Lights and shadows](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/lights_and_shadows.html)), plus shadows off and volumetric fog energy 0 ([Volumetric fog](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/volumetric_fog.html)). Forward+ handles 512 clustered lights in view by default, so the cost is in shadows, not light count ([Lights and shadows](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/lights_and_shadows.html)).

Godot's built-in occlusion culling is a weaker fit than it sounds. It rasterizes OccluderInstance3D shapes on the CPU with Embree, and it gains less in Forward+ because Forward+ already runs a depth prepass ([Godot: Occlusion culling](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/occlusion_culling.html)). Baking skips terrain built by addons, and moving occluders forces a costly BVH rebuild ([same source](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/occlusion_culling.html)). `ArrayOccluder3D` can be filled from script, so coarse occluders per solid voxel region are possible. Their rebuild cost per dig is unmeasured. Rooms and Portals existed only in Godot 3.x ([godot-proposals #3920](https://github.com/godotengine/godot-proposals/issues/3920)).

The best fit for an underground game is **Minecraft's Advanced Cave Culling**. Hardware occlusion queries stalled the GPU, and checking for fully solid chunk walls culled almost nothing (about 1 chunk in 100). So Mojang stored 15 bits per 16³ section: one bit for each pair of faces that a flood fill through open space can connect. It costs **0.1 to 0.2 ms to rebuild after a block change**, runs in the background, and culls **50 to 99% of geometry** ([Tomcc: Advanced Cave Culling](https://tomcc.github.io/2014/08/31/visibility-1.html)). In this game, that becomes a per-mesh-block connectivity table, updated on dig. Each frame, a breadth-first walk starts from the camera's block and hides mesh blocks and their decoration chunks that cannot be reached through open faces in the view direction. Voxel Tools' `mesh_block_entered`/`exited` notifications give the block list. In GDScript this is plausible for the ~4,000 blocks in view, but it has to be measured. It is a medium-term project because it touches terrain, decorations and lights together.

## Loading and physics hold the biggest remaining CPU wins

The 80 s load is a GDScript maths problem. One 16³ block holds 4,096 voxels. At about 40 µs per cave-shape query, a block with no shortcuts costs about 164 ms. A 128-voxel view distance covers about 4,096 blocks. That works out to roughly 85 s on 8 threads, which matches what the project measured. (This is the research team's arithmetic, not a sourced figure.) Voxel Tools' own advice lines up with it: return early on uniform blocks with `fill_f`, move fixed maths out of inner loops, and avoid heavy 3D loops in GDScript ([Voxel Tools: Scripting](https://voxel-tools.readthedocs.io/en/latest/scripting/); [Performance](https://voxel-tools.readthedocs.io/en/latest/performance/)). **Already done:** `cave_maker.gd` fills solid-rock blocks at once, and since F211 it measures blocks near caves on a coarse 2 m net before refining. Planning for cave life, plants, roots and crystals runs on `WorkerThreadPool` behind a moving loading screen. Plants dropped from 22 s to 17.5 s when split across threads.

The next steps follow in order of payoff. **Cache the plan**: planning is seeded and deterministic, so save its output as packed arrays to `user://cache/<seed>_<version>.bin` and skip planning on the next load. **Save terrain too**: the project uses `VoxelStreamMemory`, which keeps nothing. `VoxelStreamSQLite` with `save_generator_output` stores generated blocks, "the way Minecraft works," so the second load reads from disk ([Voxel Tools: Streams](https://voxel-tools.readthedocs.io/en/latest/streams/)). **Use group tasks**: `add_group_task()` spreads per-region work across all workers and reports progress through `get_group_processed_element_count()` ([Godot: WorkerThreadPool](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_workerthreadpool.html)). Each region writes only its own slot and seeds its own RNG, so co-op peers still get identical worlds ([Thread-safe APIs](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/performance/thread_safe_apis.html)). **Measure in an exported build**: threaded GDScript is much slower in the editor than in exported builds ([Voxel Tools: Performance](https://voxel-tools.readthedocs.io/en/latest/performance/)), so the 17.5 s figure may already be pessimistic.

Static typing is cheap insurance on every hot path. Typed instructions made operators 25 to 50% faster and native method calls 70 to 150% faster in the GDScript developer's benchmarks ([Godot blog: typed instructions](https://godotengine.org/article/gdscript-progress-report-typed-instructions)). The long-term fix is native code. Vendor and community tests report 5x to 20x gains for pure maths ([Jenova benchmark](https://jenova-framework.github.io/docs/pages/Misc/Benchmark)), but treat those as rough. VoxelGeneratorGraph runs at "similar speed to C++ generators" and can skip whole blocks with range analysis ([Voxel Tools: Generators](https://voxel-tools.readthedocs.io/en/latest/generators/)). It handles only basic operations, though, so the room-and-tunnel layout would have to be expressed as graph nodes. The other option is a small godot-cpp GDExtension that takes packed arrays in and gives SDF values back. One thing is unconfirmed: whether a custom C++ Voxel Tools generator can be built against the GDExtension edition.

Terrain collision is the other loading cost. Creating per-block trimesh colliders costs **3 to 5 times as much as meshing** and slows loading "significantly" ([Voxel Tools: Performance](https://voxel-tools.readthedocs.io/en/latest/performance/)). Since 1.4, "collision-only viewers no longer create visual meshes" ([Changelog](https://voxel-tools.readthedocs.io/en/latest/changelog/)). So give each player one visual viewer at 128 with `requires_collisions = false`, plus a collision-only viewer at about 32 to 48. **Partly done:** `main.gd` already gives remote players a 48-voxel viewer without visuals, but the local viewer still asks for collision across all 128. During the loading screen, `voxel/threads/main/time_budget_ms` (default about 8 ms) can be raised and then lowered again for play ([same source](https://voxel-tools.readthedocs.io/en/latest/performance/)). Keep `mesh_block_size` at 16, because 32 cuts draw calls but makes digging slower ([same source](https://voxel-tools.readthedocs.io/en/latest/performance/)).

Physics is a one-setting experiment. Jolt has been built in since 4.4 and became the default for new 3D projects in 4.6, but existing projects keep Godot Physics ([Godot 4.6 release](https://godotengine.org/releases/4.6/)). No published benchmark with numbers exists for 4.4 to 4.7, so test it on a branch: set `physics/3d/physics_engine` to Jolt and measure the same 110-body debris scene. Its edge handling helps bodies slide over trimesh terrain ([Godot: Using Jolt](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/physics/using_jolt_physics.html)). One caveat: Voxel Tools reports that Jolt moves collider setup onto the main thread when terrain shapes enter the tree. That comes from a search snippet, so measure chunk loading too. Whichever engine wins, the debris recipe is the same. Pool 30 to 50 bodies and recycle the oldest. Use box or sphere shapes. Put debris on a layer that hits only terrain. Sleep pieces quickly, then turn sleeping ones into plain meshes. Keep 60 Hz ticks, and use physics interpolation instead of more ticks ([Godot: CPU optimization](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/performance/cpu_optimization.html)).

## Co-op stays cheap if digs travel as commands, not chunks

Steam's networking defaults clamp each connection to **256 KB/s**, with messages up to 512 KB and a 5 ms Nagle delay ([Valve GameNetworkingSockets](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ValveSoftware/GameNetworkingSockets/master/include/steam/steamnetworkingtypes.h)). Valheim's real-world level is about 150 KB/s, according to community analysis ([Edgegap](https://edgegap.com/blog/valheim-multiplayer-game-backend-deep-dive)). A dig sent as "sphere at x,y,z, radius, tool, edit id" is about 20 to 30 bytes. That fits the project's request → host approves → everyone updates rule, and every peer gets the same result from the same seed. Voxel Tools' synchronizer works only with VoxelTerrain and is "very experimental" ([Voxel Tools: Multiplayer](https://voxel-tools.readthedocs.io/en/latest/multiplayer/)). That is one more reason to stay on VoxelTerrain rather than VoxelLodTerrain, which also does not cache generated blocks ([Generators](https://voxel-tools.readthedocs.io/en/latest/generators/)). Send each dig only to peers who have that area loaded, using `get_viewer_network_peer_ids_in_area()` ([VoxelTerrain API](https://voxel-tools.readthedocs.io/en/latest/api/VoxelTerrain/)). When a player joins, send only the edited blocks, compressed, on their own channel. Merge edits per block instead of keeping a growing list, which is the lesson from Valheim's terrain rework ([Valheim patch](https://www.essentiallysports.com/esports-news-valheim-iron-gate-studio-rolls-new-patch-to-rework-terrain-upgrade-hoe-and-more)). Separate `transfer_channel`s keep a big reliable transfer from stalling movement ([Godot: High-level multiplayer](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/networking/high_level_multiplayer.html)).

## Conclusion

The research changes the framing of the problem. For a cave game, "draw only what you look at" is really "draw only what the tunnels let you see." Godot already does the looking part, and the tunnel part is a custom system worth building once the cheap wins are in. The other shift: on the owner's PC the frame budget is no longer the main constraint. Load time and the lack of graphics settings are what stand between the game and Valheim-class minimum specs.

There are two big unknowns, and only measurement settles them. One is how much Forward+ at Potato settings really costs on an integrated GPU. The other is whether Jolt helps or hurts once terrain colliders are counted. Both are cheap to test. Both should be tested before larger rewrites, so the long-term native-code work goes where the numbers point.
