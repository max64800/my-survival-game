# Low-end PC support, performance measurement, and co-op network budgets (Godot 4.7)

## What counts as low-end hardware today, and what similar games list as minimum specs

### Takeaway
In the October 2026 Steam survey, the typical player has an RTX 3060/4060-class GPU, 8 to 16 GB VRAM, 16 to 32 GB RAM, 6 to 8 CPU cores, and a 1080p screen. The low end that is still common is a GTX 1650 / RTX 3050 class card, Intel Iris Xe integrated graphics, 4 to 6 GB VRAM, 8 GB RAM, 4 cores, and the Steam Deck (4-core Zen 2, 1.6 TFLOPS RDNA2, 1280x800). Similar survival games ask for a GTX 950 / GTX 460-class card and 8 GB RAM as their minimum.

Not checked against the official store page.

### Inferences
- A sensible "potato" target for this game: GTX 1050 Ti / GTX 1650 or Intel Iris Xe / AMD Radeon 680M-class integrated graphics, 4 GB VRAM, 8 GB RAM, a 4-core CPU, 1080p output with 50 to 67% render scale, aiming for 30 FPS at Low. The Steam Deck makes a good "middle-low" target: 1280x800 at 30 to 60 FPS on Low/Medium.
- The minimum spec for Valheim (GTX 950, 8 GB, quad core) is a realistic, honest number to aim for. Core Keeper's GTX 460 is lower, but Core Keeper is a 2D-looking top-down game, so it isn't a fair comparison.
- The dev PC (RTX 3060 Ti, ~5 ms) is roughly 4 to 6 times faster than a GTX 1650 / Iris Xe on the GPU side (this ratio is a general estimate, not a sourced benchmark). So 5 ms on the dev PC could mean roughly 20 to 30 ms on a potato at the same settings and resolution. That is why scaling and quality settings are needed.
- RAM (the computer's short-term memory) matters on integrated GPUs and the Deck, because the GPU shares system RAM. With 8 GB total, the game plus Windows should stay under about 4 to 5 GB.

### Gaps
- The survey page I read did not give overall NVIDIA/AMD/Intel GPU vendor shares or a total "integrated graphics" share. Iris Xe at 1.73% is the only integrated GPU in the top 15. The total integrated share is probably higher (AMD "Radeon Graphics" iGPUs, Intel UHD), but I found no source for it.
- Official Steam-store minimum specs for Deep Rock Galactic, Enshrouded and 7 Days to Die were not fetched (the store API call failed). Only Valheim and Core Keeper were verified from Steam.
- No source found on the share of Steam Deck players for indie survival games specifically.

## Godot 4 renderers on weak hardware: Forward+ vs Mobile vs Compatibility, and fallback

### Takeaway
Forward+ has the highest base cost and is the slowest on old and low-end GPUs. Mobile is cheaper and often faster on integrated GPUs. Compatibility (OpenGL 3) is the cheapest and runs on the oldest hardware, but it drops volumetric fog, SDFGI, SSIL, SSR, TAA/FSR2 and decals, and it handles many lights badly. Since Godot 4.4 the engine automatically falls back to Compatibility when neither Vulkan nor D3D12 works. You can also pick the renderer at startup with `--rendering-method`.

### Cited Findings
- The three renderers: Forward+ (desktop only, the default on desktop; Vulkan/D3D12/Metal through RenderingDevice); Mobile (fewer features, faster on simple scenes; same backends); Compatibility (OpenGL; for low-end desktop and mobile; the default on web) — [Godot docs: Overview of renderers](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/rendering/renderers.html)
- "On old or low-end hardware, Mobile is slower than Compatibility, and Forward+ is the slowest of the three." Cost model: Compatibility has a low base cost but a high scaling cost; Mobile is medium; Forward+ has the highest base cost but a low scaling cost (it scales best as you add lights) — [Godot docs: renderers](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/rendering/renderers.html)
- Light limits: Compatibility has 8 omni/spot lights **per mesh** (can be raised); Mobile has 8 per mesh and 256 per view; Forward+ has 512 per cluster (can be raised). DirectionalLight is 8 in all three — [Godot docs: renderers](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/rendering/renderers.html)
- Missing in Compatibility: PCSS soft shadows, light projectors, VoxelGI, SDFGI, SSIL, **volumetric fog**, SSR, CompositorEffects, TAA, **FSR2**, FXAA, SMAA, SSS, compute shaders, debanding, **decals**, particle trails, DOF, VRS, HDR output. SSAO works in Compatibility but **not** in Mobile. Volumetric fog is missing from both Mobile and Compatibility — [Godot docs: renderers](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/rendering/renderers.html)
- In Compatibility, "lights with shadows use a multi-pass approach and less accurate blending" — [Godot docs: renderers (via search summary)](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/rendering/renderers.html)
- The Mobile method "can still perform better than Forward+ in simple scenes thanks to its lower complexity and lower bandwidth usage. This is especially noticeable on low-end GPUs, integrated graphics or in VR applications" — [Godot docs: Internal rendering architecture (4.7)](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/4.7/engine_details/architecture/internal_rendering_architecture.html)
- HDR 2D "increases bandwidth usage and can reduce performance on mobile GPUs or integrated graphics" — [Godot docs: Internal rendering architecture](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/latest/engine_details/architecture/internal_rendering_architecture.html)
- Automatic fallback (Godot 4.4+): if Vulkan fails on Forward+/Mobile, the engine tries Direct3D 12 and vice versa. If neither works, it falls back to Compatibility. "The project still runs, but it may look different." You can turn this off with Project Settings > Rendering > Rendering Device > "Fallback to OpenGL 3" — [Godot docs: renderers](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/rendering/renderers.html)
- Command-line choice: `--rendering-method <forward_plus|mobile|gl_compatibility>` ("Requires driver support"), `--rendering-driver <driver>` (for example vulkan, d3d12, opengl3), and `--gpu-index <n>` (Forward+/Mobile only; `--verbose` lists the devices). These work in release export templates — [Godot docs: Command line tutorial](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/editor/command_line_tutorial.html)
- Switching between Mobile and Forward+ "usually needs fewer adjustments" than switching to or from Compatibility — [Godot docs: renderers](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/rendering/renderers.html)
- A forum user on an Intel UHD 620 laptop reported poor performance even with Compatibility (2D TileMapLayer case, no fix confirmed) — [Godot Forum](https://forum.godotengine.org/t/poor-performance-godot-4-4-2d-compatibility/116701)
- Intel Arc B580 users saw slow Vulkan initialisation in the Godot 4.4.1 editor on all three renderers. The suggested workaround was the D3D12 driver — [Intel Community](https://community.intel.com/t5/Developing-Games-on-Intel/B580-Vulkan-Layer-s-Slow-Initialization/m-p/1701902)
- Compatibility has the lowest colour precision (it can show banding), and Mobile uses lower precision than Forward+ — [Godot docs: 3D rendering limitations](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/3d_rendering_limitations.html)

### Inferences
- This game depends on many small lights, fog and glowing things in dark caves, so Compatibility's 8-lights-per-mesh limit is a big problem. Large voxel terrain chunk meshes would each be touched by many lights, so lights would pop on and off. Volumetric fog would also be lost. Compatibility should be a last-resort "it at least starts" path, not the main potato mode.
- Best plan: ship Forward+ as the default. Add a "Renderer" option (Forward+ / Mobile / Compatibility) that is saved to a settings file and applied on the next launch. The renderer can't be switched while the game runs, so it must be set through a startup override (for example a launcher option or a `override.cfg` that sets `rendering/renderer/rendering_method`). This is a standard Godot mechanism, but I did not verify it in this research session. Keep the automatic OpenGL fallback switched on.
- A potato preset on Forward+ (low render scale, no volumetric fog, no SSAO/SSIL/SDFGI, few shadowed lights) is probably the better low-end path than Mobile, because it keeps the look consistent. Test both on real iGPU hardware before deciding.
- Fog for the low-end path: replace volumetric fog with plain depth/height fog from the Environment. That works in all renderers, so caves still read as foggy.
- On Windows, offer D3D12 vs Vulkan as a hidden or advanced option for Intel GPU problems.

### Gaps
- No public benchmark found comparing Forward+, Mobile and Compatibility for the same 3D scene on Intel Iris Xe or AMD iGPUs. The owner needs to measure this.
- I did not verify which Godot 4.7-specific renderer changes (if any) affect low-end performance.
- I did not verify how Voxel Tools behaves under Compatibility (its shaders and features may need RenderingDevice).

## A graphics settings menu: which options matter, presets, auto-detect

### Takeaway
The biggest levers in Godot are 3D render scale (with bilinear, FSR 1.0 or FSR 2.2 upscaling), shadows (count, atlas size, filter quality), expensive screen effects (SSAO, SSIL, SSR, SDFGI, volumetric fog, glow), view distance, and the FPS cap and vsync. Use presets (Potato/Low/Medium/High/Custom) plus a quick benchmark on first launch that saves the result and drops one tier if the FPS is too low.

### Cited Findings
- Godot 3D scaling modes: Bilinear (the default and fallback, all renderers), FSR 1.0 (Forward+ only, best combined with TAA or MSAA), FSR 2.2 (Godot 4.2+, Forward+ only, slowest and highest quality, includes its own TAA) — [Godot docs: Resolution scaling](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/resolution_scaling.html)
- Scale applies per axis: 0.5 at 1080p renders 960x540 (0.52 MPix vs 2.07 MPix), so about 4 times fewer pixels. Common values: 0.50, 0.67, 0.75, 0.85. Values above 1.0 are supersampling, "very expensive and not recommended" — [Godot docs: Resolution scaling](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/resolution_scaling.html)
- "On very slow GPUs, FSR 1.0's cost may outweigh its benefit, so bilinear may be the better choice." On slow GPUs FSR 2.2 may cost too much. Resolution scaling "shouldn't replace lowering graphics settings. Expose both in your menus." — [Godot docs: Resolution scaling](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/resolution_scaling.html)
- Settings: Rendering > Scaling 3D > Scale / Scaling mode / FSR Sharpness (0.0 sharpest, 2.0 least, default 0.2). Runtime property `Viewport.scaling_3d_scale`. Godot applies a negative mipmap bias automatically below 1.0 — [Godot docs: Resolution scaling](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/resolution_scaling.html)
- "If lowering scale barely helps, the bottleneck is probably elsewhere, such as too many draw calls (a CPU bottleneck) or costly effects like SDFGI, SSAO, or SSR" — [Godot docs: Resolution scaling](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/3d/resolution_scaling.html)
- Shadows are expensive. Smaller shadow maps help, and "disabling shadows on as many lights and objects as possible is the best improvement". Small or distant omni/spot lights lose little visually without shadows — [Godot docs: GPU optimization](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/performance/gpu_optimization.html)
- Transparency is costly for fill rate. Keep transparent areas small — [Godot docs: GPU optimization](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/performance/gpu_optimization.html)
- Reuse materials: "20,000 objects sharing 100 materials render much faster than 20,000 unique materials" — [Godot docs: GPU optimization](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/performance/gpu_optimization.html)
- RenderingServer exposes global quality calls you can wire to menu options: `directional_shadow_atlas_set_size(size, is_16bits)`, `directional_soft_shadow_filter_set_quality(quality)`, `environment_set_ssao_quality(quality, half_size, ...)`, `environment_set_volumetric_fog_volume_size(size, depth)`, `environment_glow_set_use_bicubic_upscale(enable)`, `gi_set_use_half_resolution(bool)`. It also exposes `get_video_adapter_name()`, `get_video_adapter_vendor()` and `get_video_adapter_type()` (returns RenderingDevice DeviceType, e.g. integrated) for auto-detect — [Godot docs: RenderingServer](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_renderingserver.html)
- Auto-detect from a benchmark: Unreal "can auto-detect optimal settings per player based on a quick CPU and GPU benchmark". A developer guide suggests running it during a hidden loading screen, dropping one tier if the measured FPS falls short, and saving the result so it only runs once — [Tom Looman: Auto-detect graphics settings](https://tomlooman.com/auto-detect-graphics-settings-ue4), [Tom Looman: optimal scalability settings](https://tomlooman.com/unreal-engine-optimal-graphics-settings/)
- A suggested menu layout: a Quality dropdown with Auto, Safe Mode, Low through Ultra, and Custom, where Custom unlocks individual sliders — [Bugnet: graceful degradation for low-end devices](https://bugnet.io/blog/how-to-implement-graceful-degradation-mode-for-low-end-devices)
- Gears Tactics is held up as a model graphics menu (PC Gamer). It shows performance impact per option — [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/gears-tactics-has-the-best-graphics-option-menus-ive-ever-seen-and-every-game-should-copy-them)
- Motion blur, film grain and chromatic aberration usually sit at the bottom of the menu, and players often disable them — [TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/best-pc-graphics-options-tips-tricks/)
- Auto-detect tools tend to favour visual quality over steady performance — [Switchblade Gaming](https://www.switchbladegaming.com/game-settings/universal-template/)

### Inferences
- Suggested options for this game, roughly in order of impact:
  1. **Render scale** (50 to 100%) + **upscaler** (Bilinear / FSR 1 / FSR 2). Default to FSR 1 at 67 to 77% on Low/Potato, and bilinear on very weak iGPUs.
  2. **Shadows**: Off / Low (only the player's lantern casts shadows, small atlas) / Medium / High. Also limit the **number of shadow-casting lights**. Glowing crystals, mushrooms and so on should never cast shadows.
  3. **Light count / light range**: a cap on how many small decorative lights are active. Fade out distant ones (for example `distance_fade` on lights, a Godot feature not checked here) or swap them for emissive-only materials.
  4. **Volumetric fog**: Off (use plain depth/height fog) / Low (small volume size) / High.
  5. **SSAO / SSIL / SDFGI / SSR**: on or off each. All off on Low.
  6. **Glow**: on/off (cheap-ish, but adds up on an iGPU).
  7. **View distance**: Voxel Tools viewer distance + LOD + fog end distance. This also helps CPU and memory.
  8. **Decoration density** (cave clutter, grass, particles).
  9. **Anti-aliasing**: Off / FXAA / TAA / MSAA 2x/4x. FSR2 replaces TAA.
  10. **Max FPS** (30/60/120/unlimited), **VSync** (on/off/adaptive), window mode, resolution.
- Presets: Potato, Low, Medium, High, Ultra, Custom. On first launch, run a 5 to 10 second benchmark (a hidden fly-through in a cave scene), pick a preset, then save it. Offer a "Safe mode" launch option (Compatibility + Potato) for when the game crashes or shows a black screen.
- The game should show a small "this option costs a lot / a little" label per option (the Gears Tactics idea), which also suits the owner's simple-explanation style.

### Gaps
- No authoritative Godot-specific "graphics menu" guide was found. The GodotPrompter `rendering-pipeline` skill (local) covers graphics settings menus and may help with implementation.
- No measured per-option costs in Godot (for example "volumetric fog costs X ms on an iGPU"). These must be measured in this project.

## Measuring performance: profilers, monitors, frame-time logging, automated benchmarks, testing on weak hardware

### Takeaway
Use Godot's Debugger (Profiler, Visual Profiler, Monitors) during development. Add `Performance.add_custom_monitor` counters for game-specific things (active lights, loaded chunks, monsters). Log per-frame CPU/GPU times to a file during an automated fly-through benchmark run from the command line. Use the official godot-benchmarks project as a model for the metrics (Render CPU, Render GPU, Idle, Physics).

### Cited Findings
- Custom monitors: `Performance.add_custom_monitor("category/name", callable)`. The callable must return a number >= 0. It shows up under Debugger > Monitors (queried once per second in the editor) and can be read in game with `Performance.get_custom_monitor()`. It works in exported debug **and release** builds — [Godot docs: Custom performance monitors](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/scripting/debug/custom_performance_monitors.html)
- Command-line tools for testing (all in release templates unless noted): `--print-fps`; `--fixed-fps <fps>`; `--disable-vsync`; `--max-fps <fps>`; `--frame-delay <ms>` ("Simulate high CPU load"); `--time-scale`; `--resolution WxH`; `--gpu-profile` ("Show a GPU profile of the tasks that took the most time during frame rendering"); `--gpu-abort` (debug/editor only); `--write-movie <file>` (a deterministic, fixed-time capture); `--benchmark` / `--benchmark-file <path>` (editor builds only: startup/run time as JSON); `--headless`; `--audio-driver` — [Godot docs: Command line tutorial](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/editor/command_line_tutorial.html)
- godot-benchmarks (official): run with `--run-benchmarks`, `--include-benchmarks="glob"`, `--save-json="path"` after `--`. Each scene runs for 5 s and records per-frame averages: **Render CPU** (draw-call setup, excluding scripts/physics), **Render GPU**, **Idle** (process functions), **Physics**, and **Main Thread Time** (setup/load). Results are shown at benchmarks.godotengine.org — [godot-benchmarks on GitHub](https://github.com/godotengine/godot-benchmarks)
- RenderingServer timing APIs: `get_frame_setup_time_cpu()`, `get_rendering_info(info)` (draw calls, video memory and so on through the RenderingInfo enum), plus the `viewport_*` measure-render-time functions — [Godot docs: RenderingServer](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_renderingserver.html) (descriptions of the viewport timing functions were not in the part of the page I read)
- Fill-rate test: disable V-Sync and compare FPS in a large window vs a tiny one. A big gain means you are fill-rate (pixel) bound, and little gain means the bottleneck is elsewhere (CPU/draw calls) — [Godot docs: GPU optimization](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/performance/gpu_optimization.html)
- Built-in game benchmarks should be repeatable and representative (Tom's Hardware piece on good benchmarks) — [Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/uk/news/what-makes-a-good-game-benchmark)

### Inferences
- A concrete in-project setup:
  1. An autoload `PerfLog` that, each frame, records `Performance.get_monitor(TIME_PROCESS / TIME_PHYSICS_PROCESS)`, the viewport's measured CPU/GPU render time (after turning on `RenderingServer.viewport_set_measure_render_time`), draw calls, video memory, and custom counters (active lights, loaded voxel blocks, monsters, network bytes/s). At the end it writes CSV/JSON with average, 1% low and 99th-percentile frame time.
  2. A `--benchmark-cave` user argument (read via `OS.get_cmdline_user_args()`) that loads a fixed seed, flies the camera on a recorded path through the busiest cavern for 60 s, then quits. Run it muted (`--audio-driver Dummy`, as the project rules require), with `--disable-vsync`, at fixed resolutions, on each preset.
  3. A regression check: compare results to a saved baseline and fail if the 99th-percentile frame time grows by more than ~10%. GPU numbers on CI runners without GPUs are meaningless, so run GPU benchmarks on the dev PC (a local "nightly" task). On CI, run only CPU-side checks (script time, meshing time, `--headless`).
- Cheap ways to test weak hardware: (a) on the dev PC, set the 3D scale and force the Potato preset, then use `--frame-delay` to fake a slow CPU and `--max-fps` to cap; (b) use the Windows GPU selector / `--gpu-index` to run on a laptop iGPU if one is available; (c) buy a used laptop with Intel Iris Xe or UHD 620 (cheap second-hand); (d) a Steam Deck is the most valuable single test device (fixed hardware, large share of Linux players); (e) NVIDIA's control panel/Afterburner can lower GPU power and clocks for a rough "slower GPU" test (a common practice, not sourced here).
- Outside tools: RenderDoc works with Vulkan and D3D12 for single-frame captures. NVIDIA Nsight Graphics works on the RTX 3060 Ti. PIX works for D3D12. These are standard tools but were not checked for Godot 4.7 specifics in this session.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch the Godot Profiler / Visual Profiler doc page, or check RenderDoc / Nsight / PIX usage with Godot 4.7. Those claims are from general knowledge and are marked as such above.
- No source found for an established Godot CI performance-test setup (for example a GitHub Actions GPU runner).

## Multiplayer: bandwidth and tick rate for 4-player co-op, voxel edit sync, Godot multiplayer costs, Steam networking limits

### Takeaway
Steam networking can send messages up to 512 KB. Its default send-rate clamp is 256 KB/s per connection and the Nagle delay defaults to 5 ms. Valheim, a successful co-op survival game, runs at roughly 10 KB in flight per peer and about 150 KB/s. For this game, aim for a host upload of about 20 to 40 KB/s per client in normal play (under ~150 KB/s total for 3 clients). Send voxel **edits** (small, reliable), not chunks, and let clients generate unedited chunks from the shared world seed. Voxel Tools has an experimental server-authoritative synchronizer that does exactly this.

### Cited Findings
- Steam networking (GameNetworkingSockets headers): `k_cbMaxSteamNetworkingSocketsMessageSizeSend = 512 * 1024` (512 KiB max per message). Send flags: Unreliable = 0 ("Can be lost"), NoNagle = 1, NoDelay = 4, Reliable = 8 (up to the max size), UseCurrentThread = 16. SendRateMin/SendRateMax: "Minimum/maximum send rate clamp, in bytes/sec. The default value is 256K." SendBufferSize default "512k (524288 bytes)". NagleTime "Default is 5000us (5ms)". MTU_DataSize is the largest message that won't fragment — [steamnetworkingtypes.h (Valve GameNetworkingSockets)](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ValveSoftware/GameNetworkingSockets/master/include/steam/steamnetworkingtypes.h)
- ISteamNetworkingMessages: reliable messages on the same channel are delivered at most once and in order. Unreliable ones may be dropped, reordered or duplicated. There is no ordering between channels. Small channel numbers are most efficient, and all channels to one user share one connection. It sits on ISteamNetworkingSockets and can use Steam Datagram Relay — [Steamworks docs: ISteamNetworkingMessages](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/api/ISteamNetworkingMessages)
- Godot `@rpc` options: mode `authority`/`any_peer`; `call_local`/`call_remote`; transfer_mode `unreliable`, `unreliable_ordered` (drops late packets), `reliable` ("has a significant performance penalty"); transfer_channel (last argument). Channels are separate streams, so a blocked reliable stream (for example chat) doesn't stall gameplay. The default channel 0 is really three channels, one per transfer mode. UDP MTU is only a few hundred bytes, so large messages are split and reassembled — [Godot docs: High-level multiplayer](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/networking/high_level_multiplayer.html)
- MultiplayerSynchronizer: `replication_interval` (default 0.0 = every network process frame) for "always" properties, and `delta_interval` for "on change" properties. The docs give no measured overhead — [Godot 4.2 docs: MultiplayerSynchronizer](https://godot.readthedocs.io/en/4.2/classes/class_multiplayersynchronizer.html), [Godot 4.x docs](https://docs.godotengine.org/de/4.x/classes/class_multiplayersynchronizer.html)
- Voxel Tools multiplayer (experimental): the server is authoritative. `VoxelTerrainMultiplayerSynchronizer` (from 2023/04/13) uses Godot's high-level RPCs. The server creates a `VoxelViewer` per player with `network_peer_id` and `requires_data_block_notifications`. Clients keep a local VoxelViewer. Blocks are packed with `VoxelBlockSerializer`. If a block isn't edited, the server can tell the client to generate it locally. Edits are sent as a bounding box only to peers whose viewers are near (`get_viewer_network_peer_ids_in_area()`), and the older scripted approach uses `area_edit_notification_enabled` + `VoxelTool.paste`. Limits: `VoxelLodTerrain` is not supported yet; "RPCs use UDP, so sending large voxel volumes may be slow"; `try_set_block_data` cancels blocks still loading. Planned: block versioning/caching, and diffing with a 1-bit change map that sends only the changed part when under 30% of a block changed — [Voxel Tools docs: Multiplayer](https://voxel-tools.readthedocs.io/en/latest/multiplayer/)
- Valheim (community analysis, not official): ZDOMan.SendZDOs keeps at most 10 KB in flight per peer, so throughput is about 10 KB / RTT. Steam's send rate is pinned at 150 KB/s (min = max, which turns off Steam's own rate estimator). That gives about 68 KB/s at 150 ms RTT. Mods raise the limit to 128 to 640 KB/s per player, and community testing saw problems near 5 to 6 players — [FiresGhettoNetworking (Thunderstore)](https://thunderstore.io/c/valheim/p/VerdantsAscent/FiresGhettoNetworking/v/1.2.1/), [Supercraft wiki: Better Networking](https://supercraft.host/wiki/valheim/better-networking), [Edgegap: Valheim backend deep dive](https://edgegap.com/blog/valheim-multiplayer-game-backend-deep-dive)

### Inferences
- Suggested budget for this game (host = one player's home PC, often on a home connection with limited upload):
  - **Tick rate**: simulate on the host at the physics rate (60 Hz). Send player and monster positions at 20 Hz (unreliable_ordered), with interpolation on clients. Send slow-changing things (health, inventory, doors, stations) on change only (reliable).
  - **Per client**: player state ~20 Hz x ~40 bytes x 4 players + monsters (for example 20 nearby x 20 Hz x ~24 bytes) is roughly 13 to 15 KB/s, plus overhead, so about 20 to 40 KB/s per client. With 3 remote clients the host uploads about 60 to 120 KB/s. That is under Steam's default 256 KB/s clamp and close to Valheim's real-world level. These are my own estimates, not sourced figures.
  - **Join / catch-up**: the big cost is sending edited chunks to a joining player. Rely on the shared seed (clients generate untouched chunks themselves) and send only edited blocks (compressed with Godot's `PackedByteArray.compress()` using zstd or deflate; very effective on voxel data, which repeats a lot). Spread the sending over several seconds (for example 64 to 128 KB/s) so it doesn't push out live gameplay traffic, and keep it on its own channel.
- **Voxel edits**: send "dig sphere at (x,y,z), radius r, tool id, edit id" (~20 to 30 bytes, reliable) instead of voxel data. The host checks it, applies it, and sends it to all peers. This fits the project's "request, host approves, everyone updates" rule. Because Voxel Tools edits are deterministic given the same input, every peer gets the same result. Keep an edit log (or per-block version numbers) so a reconnecting player can catch up.
- **Godot costs**: avoid one MultiplayerSynchronizer per small object at 60 Hz. Each adds per-frame work and packet overhead. Use a few batched "snapshot" RPCs (pack many entities into one PackedByteArray at 20 Hz), and set `replication_interval` / `delta_interval` to 0.05 s on the Synchronizers you do use. Use separate `transfer_channel`s for gameplay, voxel edits, chunk streaming and chat, so a big reliable chunk transfer doesn't block movement.
- **Steam**: GodotSteam's SteamMultiplayerPeer (GDExtension) plugs Steam networking into Godot's high-level multiplayer, so the same RPCs work. Keep single messages well under 512 KB. Raise SendRateMax only if join-streaming is too slow. Measure bytes/s with a custom monitor.
